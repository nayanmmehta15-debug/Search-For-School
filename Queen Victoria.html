I magine waking up one morning as an ordinary eighteen-year-old and being told that you are now the queen of the whole country. That is exactly what happened to Queen Victoria. She went on to reign for more than sixty-three years, which was longer than any British monarch before her. Her time on the throne was so remarkable that we still call it the Victorian era today. In this page, I am going to tell you all about her extraordinary life, from her lonely childhood to her magnificent funeral.

A Lonely Childhood

Victoria was born on 24 May 1819 at Kensington Palace in London. Her full name was Alexandrina Victoria. She was named Alexandrina after her godfather, the Tsar of Russia, and Victoria after her mother.

Her father, Prince Edward, the Duke of Kent, died when she was only eight months old, so she was brought up by her mother, who came from Germany. Victoria grew up speaking German as well as English.

You might think that growing up as a princess would be glamorous, but Victoria's childhood was strict and rather lonely. Her mother and a man called Sir John Conroy invented a set of rules known as the Kensington System.

Under these rules, Victoria hardly ever met other children. She had to sleep in her mother's bedroom every night, and she was not even allowed to walk down the stairs without holding someone's hand.

Her main companions were her kind governess, Baroness Lehzen, her pet spaniel, Dash, and her collection of more than 130 wooden dolls, which she dressed in tiny costumes.

Becoming Queen

At about six o'clock in the morning on 20 June 1837, Victoria was woken up and told that her uncle, King William IV, had died.

The Archbishop of Canterbury and the Lord Chamberlain had rushed to Kensington Palace to give her the news. At just eighteen years old, she was now the queen.

Legend says that she came downstairs in her dressing gown with her hair hanging loose.

Victoria was determined to show that she was ready.

Her very first decision was to be called Victoria instead of Alexandrina.

She also asked for an hour on her own, and soon afterwards she moved her bed out of her mother's room. It was her first taste of independence.

A few weeks later, she became the first monarch to live at Buckingham Palace.

Her coronation took place at Westminster Abbey on 28 June 1838, when she was nineteen. Crowds packed the streets of London to see her, and it was a glorious day.

At first, Victoria relied on the prime minister, Lord Melbourne, who taught her how the government worked.

Love and Family

In 1839, Victoria's cousin, Prince Albert of Saxe-Coburg and Gotha, came to visit. She liked him immediately.

Because Victoria was queen, nobody was allowed to propose to her, so she had to ask him herself. Luckily, he said yes!

They got married on 10 February 1840. Victoria wore a white satin dress decorated with orange blossom.

Before then, brides wore all sorts of colours, but after Victoria's wedding, white became the fashionable choice, and lots of brides still choose it today.

Victoria and Albert were devoted to each other, and they had nine children.

Albert was clever, hardworking and full of ideas about science, art and education. He also helped to make Christmas trees popular in Britain.

The Great Exhibition

Prince Albert's most famous project was the Great Exhibition of 1851.

It was held in Hyde Park inside a gigantic building made of glass and iron, which people nicknamed the Crystal Palace.

Inside, there were thousands of inventions and treasures from around the world, including machines, jewels and enormous steam engines.

More than six million people visited over five months, and Victoria enjoyed it so much that she went back again and again.

A Time of Great Change

Britain changed enormously while Victoria was queen. Railways spread across the country, so people could travel faster than ever before.

In 1840, the Penny Post began, and the Penny Black, the world's first sticky postage stamp, had a picture of Victoria's head on it.

The electric telegraph let messages be sent in minutes instead of days.

Factories grew, and towns and cities became crowded.

In 1858, the River Thames smelled so terrible that people called it the Great Stink.

The engineer Joseph Bazalgette solved the problem by building a huge network of sewers, which made London much healthier. Many of those sewers are still used today.

However, not everything was wonderful. Many poor families lived in dirty, crowded houses, and thousands of children worked long hours in factories, mines and chimneys.

Slowly, new laws were passed to protect them. In 1870, the Education Act was passed to make sure there were schools for children across England and Wales.

Sadness and Mourning

In December 1861, tragedy struck. Prince Albert became seriously ill and died on 14 December at the age of only forty-two.

Victoria was heartbroken. She wore black for the rest of her life, which was about forty years, and she hardly appeared in public for a long time.

This period of mourning was so long that people began to call her the Widow of Windsor.

Gradually, Victoria returned to her duties. She loved spending time at Balmoral Castle in Scotland and Osborne House on the Isle of Wight.

She became close friends with her Scottish servant, John Brown, who cheered her up.

Later, she worked with two important prime ministers, Benjamin Disraeli and William Gladstone, although she liked one of them far more than the other.

Empress of India

During Victoria's reign, the British Empire grew to be the largest in history.

At its height, it covered about a quarter of the land on Earth and included countries such as India, Canada, Australia and parts of Africa.

In 1876, Victoria was given the extra title of Empress of India.

It is important to remember that many people in the empire did not choose to be ruled by Britain, and some of them suffered greatly.

Historians still discuss whether the empire was fair.

Grandmother of Europe

Victoria's children and grandchildren married into royal families all over Europe, which earned her the nickname the Grandmother of Europe.

Her grandchildren included Kaiser Wilhelm II of Germany, Tsarina Alexandra of Russia and King George V of Britain.

Being a queen could also be dangerous. At least seven different people tried to attack her during her life, but she survived every single time.

Two Big Celebrations

In 1887, Britain celebrated her Golden Jubilee to mark fifty years on the throne, with parties and processions across the country.

Ten years later, in 1897, came the Diamond Jubilee for sixty years, and it was even bigger.

Soldiers from all over the empire marched through London.

By then, Victoria was old and found walking difficult, so she stayed in her carriage while a special service was held outside St Paul's Cathedral.

The End of an Era

Queen Victoria died on 22 January 1901 at Osborne House, aged eighty-one.

She had reigned for 63 years and 7 months. Her son then became King Edward VII.

Why She Still Matters

Queen Victoria left behind a great deal. The Victoria Cross, the highest medal for bravery in the British armed forces, was created in 1856 and is still awarded today.

Places all over the world are named after her, such as Victoria Falls in Africa, the state of Victoria in Australia and the city of Victoria in Canada.

She also wrote in her journal almost every day from the age of thirteen, so we know an incredible amount about her thoughts and feelings.

In my opinion, Queen Victoria was a remarkable person. She began as a lonely girl who was kept away from the world, yet she became one of the most powerful and famous women in history.
